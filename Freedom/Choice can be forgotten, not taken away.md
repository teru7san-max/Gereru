# Choice can be forgotten, not taken away

> [!quote] From my notes (not checked against the book)
> I had always known logically that I could choose not to study law! But emotionally it had never been an option! That's when I realized that in sacrificing my power to choose I had made a choice — A BAD ONE.
>
> CHOICE IS AN ACTION! It's not just something we have but something we do!
>
> The ability to choose cannot be taken away or even given away — it can only be forgotten!
>
> When we surrender our ability to choose — something or someone else will step in to choose for us!

## Meaning

There's a difference between *knowing* you could choose and *feeling* that you can. The author knew he could leave law, but emotionally it was never on the table, and not choosing turned out to be a choice too. When we stop exercising choice, other people, habits or circumstances decide for us, and over time we start to believe we have no options. That's the "learned helplessness" in my notes: "Бид аажмаар 'Би сонгож чадна' гэдэг итгэлээ алддаг."

## Core insight

My ability to choose is never taken from me; I forget it, and then others choose for me.

Монголоор: *Сонгох чадварыг хэн ч булааж авч чадахгүй, зөвхөн мартаж болно.*

## Critical look

- **The research nuance supports the claim.** Maier and Seligman, the researchers behind "learned helplessness", revised their view in 2016. They now argue that passivity is the brain's *default* response to prolonged stress, and what has to be learned is the sense of control. That fits "choice can be forgotten" and suggests it has to be actively practised, not just believed.
- **Choice isn't unlimited.** Circumstances really do narrow the options: money, health, other people's needs. The useful claim is that *some* choice almost always remains, not that every situation is fully chosen.
- **It can turn into blame.** "If you didn't choose, you chose badly" is true in one sense, but it can make people feel guilty for constraints they didn't create. That's the same risk as "suffering is optional".
- **"Choose yourself, disappoint others" (#32)** is a strong slogan that needs care. It works for a reflexive "yes". It doesn't work as a general stance toward the people you love.

## Connections

- [[Pain is the first arrow, suffering is the second]]: Nguyen says how we *react* is up to us. McKeown says choice is something we *do*. Both put freedom in the response, not in the circumstances, and both share the self-blame risk above.
- **Bronnie Ware's regret, as quoted in my notes (#7):** "I wish I'd had the courage to live a life true to myself, not the life others expected of me." Forgotten choice, seen at the end of a life. This also links to `authenticity`.
- `freedom` ↔ `courage`: the book says clarity comes first and courage follows (#32). It's worth testing whether that holds for me.
