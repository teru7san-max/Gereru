# Pain is the first arrow, suffering is the second

> [!quote] Passage (exact, abridged; the full text is below)
> There is an important distinction to make when speaking about suffering. When I mention suffering in this book, I'm referring to psychological and emotional suffering. There is a way in which, no matter what happens in your life, you do not have to suffer emotionally and psychologically.
>
> I am not saying that what we go through is all in our heads or made up. Terrible and unfortunate events happen to people every single day. What I'm saying is that although we experience a lot of pain in our lives, suffering is optional. In other words, pain is unavoidable, but how we react to the events and circumstances that happen in our lives is up to us, and that will dictate whether we suffer or not.
>
> Buddhists say that anytime we experience a negative event in our lives, two arrows fly our way. Being physically struck by an arrow is painful. Being struck by a second emotional arrow is even more painful (suffering).
>
> The Buddha explained, "In life, we can't always control the first arrow. However, the second arrow is our reaction to the first. The second arrow is optional."
>
> When I first heard of this quote from Buddha a few years back, I was confounded because although I understood what he meant, I didn't know how I could apply this in my own life. […] How can I just choose not to suffer? If it were as easy as that I don't think anyone would be suffering anymore.

> [!quote]- The author's story (exact, continued)
> It wasn't until years later that I came across a new understanding of where suffering comes from that I was able to stop it at its source.
>
> As I began my journey of self-improvement, I came across a myriad of different teachings, studies, and methods to help people overcome their problems. I read dozens, if not hundreds of books, studied psychology, went to therapists, listened to many different thought leaders, tried changing my habits, waking up at 4am, changing my diet, becoming more structured and disciplined, shadow work, studying personality types, meditating daily, going on spiritual retreats, following spiritual masters, and researching different ancient religions.
>
> If you name it, I've probably tried it. I was desperate to find an answer because I wanted to know how to stop suffering in my own life as well as help others do the same. Although some of these things did help me improve incrementally, it didn't stop my suffering. I still always felt extremely anxious, fearful, unfulfilled, irritated, angry, frustrated, and heavy every day. Even after doing all of that, I still didn't discover the answer and, if I'm being honest, I was even more lost than before I started on this quest.
>
> I felt purposeless, hopeless, and directionless. I didn't know what to do anymore, where to look, or who to talk to. It wasn't until I was in my darkest hour that a glimmer of hope began to lead me to the light.
>
> All of the sudden, after years and years of searching, I stumbled upon one of my first mentors that taught me how to become a coach and he revealed to me the answer of how I can alleviate my own suffering.
>
> The answer I discovered was in the understanding of how our minds work and how the human experience is created.

The chapter opens with a Thich Nhat Hanh epigraph, which has its own note: [[We cling to familiar suffering]].

## Meaning

There are two layers to a bad experience:

1. **The first arrow (pain):** what actually happens, such as a loss, an injury, a rejection or a failure. Often you can't prevent it.
2. **The second arrow (suffering):** what the mind adds on top, such as "this shouldn't have happened", "it's my fault", "it will always be like this", replaying it, dreading what comes next.

The author's claim is that the second layer isn't fixed by circumstances, so it is possible not to suffer psychologically whatever happens. He is careful to say pain is real and not "all in your head".

Then he admits the hard part himself: knowing this didn't help him. "Just choose not to suffer" isn't an instruction anyone can follow. He says years of methods brought only incremental improvement, and that what finally worked was understanding *where suffering comes from*: how the mind creates experience. The passage promises that answer but doesn't give it yet.

## Core insight

Pain happens to me; suffering is the second arrow, and it comes from my reaction.

## Critical look

- **Well supported as a distinction.** Acceptance-based psychology makes a similar split. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), for example, separates the discomfort that comes with a situation from the extra struggle of trying to avoid or fight it, and research generally links that kind of avoidance with worse outcomes. Meditation teacher Shinzen Young puts it as a formula: suffering = pain × resistance.
- **"Optional" doesn't mean easy, and it doesn't mean blame.** If suffering is "up to us", it's easy to slide into "so my suffering is my fault", which is itself a second arrow. The author's own story shows it wasn't simply a choice for him.
- **Where does sadness go?** Grief after a loss is an emotional response, but it isn't obviously the "second arrow". It may be part of the first. The useful line probably isn't *emotion versus no emotion*. It is a feeling allowed to move through (pain) versus a feeling fed by resistance and stories (suffering). That matches the distinction in this vault between letting an emotion settle and suppressing it. "You do not have to suffer emotionally" can sound like "you shouldn't feel anything", and that isn't the healthy reading.
- **The Buddha "quote".** The two-arrows teaching is traditionally linked to the *Sallatha Sutta* (The Arrow, Saṃyutta Nikāya 36.6). The quoted line reads like a modern paraphrase rather than a translation, so treat it as Nguyen's wording of the idea, not the Buddha's words.
- **The conversion story.** "I tried everything, nothing worked, then I found *the* answer" is a common narrative arc in self-help. It may be true for him. It's still one person's account, and it sets up the rest of the book as *the* answer. Judge that answer by whether it works when you test it, not by the story.

## Connections

- **Your own `insight` vs. `active` distinction.** "Although I understood what he meant, I didn't know how I could apply this" is exactly the gap between understanding and living that the `active` tag exists for. Nguyen's whole book is framed as the bridge across it.
- [[We cling to familiar suffering]]: one reason the second arrow keeps flying is that familiar suffering feels safer than the unknown.
- `suffering` ↔ `non-resistance` ↔ `acceptance` ↔ `peace`: if suffering is the second arrow, these are candidate ways of not firing it. They are worth comparing as the book unfolds.
- **The Stoics** (Epictetus, paraphrased): we're disturbed not by things but by our judgements about them. The two say the same thing at the core, but the Stoics locate the cause in *judgement*, while Nguyen (as the passage hints) will locate it in *thinking* more broadly.
