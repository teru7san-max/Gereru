# Essentialism (Greg McKeown)

> [!summary] The book in one question
> **"What is essential? Eliminate everything else."**
> Almost everything is noise. Find the vital few, cut the rest, and make doing the essential as easy as possible.

## How the book is built

The book moves through four stages, and my notes roughly follow them:

| Stage | What it asks | My notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Essence** | Do I remember that I can choose? | #1–#16 |
| **Explore** | What actually matters? Space, play, sleep, extreme criteria | #17–#28 |
| **Eliminate** | What do I cut? Saying no, editing, limits | #29–#39 |
| **Execute** | How do I make the essential easy? Buffers, small wins, routine, the present | #40–#45 |

## Concept notes from this book

- [[Less but better]]: almost everything is noise; choose the vital few. (#1–#3, #15, #26, #35–#36)
- [[Choice can be forgotten, not taken away]]: choice is something you *do*. (#6, #13–#14, #32)
- [[Would I choose it again today]]: decide by value, not by regret. (#10, #29, #33–#34)
- [[A journal's value is in review, not volume]]: the principle behind this vault. (#21, #18)

## Critical look

- **Decision fatigue (#5)** is weaker than it sounds. Willpower depletion ("ego depletion") largely failed in large multi-lab replications, and the famous study of judges' rulings has been challenged. The practical advice (make fewer trivial decisions) can still be sensible; the science behind it is less solid.
- **"Hell yeah or no" / the 90% rule (#26)** fits *optional* opportunities. It doesn't fit constrained choices (rent, a first job, caring for someone), where a 70 can be the right answer. Applied everywhere, it can turn into maximizing, and research on maximizers versus "satisficers" (Barry Schwartz and colleagues) links maximizing with lower satisfaction. The book's own split helps: be extremely selective about *what you choose*, but "done is better than perfect" about *how you do it* (#40).
- **80/20 (#15)** is a useful heuristic, not a law of nature.
- **Bronnie Ware's regrets (#7)** come from one palliative nurse's experience, which is moving and plausible but anecdotal, not a study.
- **"Choose yourself, disappoint others" (#32)** sits in tension with `compassion` and `relationships`. Boundaries protect what matters, but not every "no" is essential. Sometimes the essential *is* the other person.
- **Saying no needs some room to say it.** The book is mostly written for people who already have more good options than time.
