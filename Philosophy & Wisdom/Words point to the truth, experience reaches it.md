# Words point to the truth, experience reaches it

> [!quote] Passage (exact)
> I want to make it clear that this book is not the only book that contains truth. The truth lies within everyone and in everything. You must look beyond the form (the physical) to see and experience the truth (the spiritual). The words in this book are not truth. They point TO the truth. Look beyond words to see the truth for yourself. Truth cannot be intellectualized; it can only be experienced. The truth lies within a feeling, which is why it cannot be formulated into a word.
>
> If you want to find truth, look beyond the words, and look for a feeling.
>
> Many who discover the truth will describe the feeling as one of complete peace, unconditional love, and an overwhelming amount of joy. People also describe it as the most familiar unfamiliar feeling. It feels like you're finally home. Look for that feeling, and everything will be revealed to you. In this book, I will not say anything that you don't already know deep inside your soul. This is why it will be a familiar unfamiliar feeling when you experience the truth.
>
> Don't try to use your intellect to figure it out — you won't. As soon as you intellectualize it, you've missed it. The truth does not come from memorizing a sentence or two.
>
> — source to confirm

## Meaning

The author is telling the reader how to read the book. Its sentences are signposts, not the destination. The "truth" meant here is not a fact you could write down and memorize. It is an experience: a felt state of peace, love and joy that seems new and yet strangely familiar, like coming home. You can't reason your way into it. The words can only point you toward noticing it for yourself.

## Core insight

Words point to the truth; only experience reaches it.

Монголоор: *Үг үнэн рүү заадаг ч, зөвхөн туршлага л түүнд хүрдэг.*

## Critical look

- **What holds up.** Some knowledge really is experiential. Knowing *about* peace differs from knowing peace, just as reading about swimming differs from swimming. Philosophy has a name for this, knowledge by acquaintance versus knowledge by description (Bertrand Russell's distinction), and contemplative traditions say much the same.
- **Two meanings of "truth".** The passage uses *truth* for an inner realization (peace, being at home). That is different from truth as "is this claim correct?" A feeling can be the right test for the first and a poor test for the second.
- **"Feels like home" isn't always a reliable signal.** Familiarity can feel like home, even familiarity with an old fear or an unhealthy relationship pattern. "Familiar unfamiliar" is the author's attempt to tell the two apart, but the passage gives no way of checking which one you're feeling.
- **The claim protects itself.** "As soon as you intellectualize it, you've missed it" means any doubt can be read as proof that you missed it. That doesn't make the claim false, but it can't be tested from the outside. The only real test is your own experience, observed honestly.
- **The framing is persuasive.** "I will not say anything you don't already know" turns agreement into recognition. Insight often does feel like recognition. It's still worth noticing when a book asks you to set your judgement aside.

## Connections

- [[We want the feeling, not the thing]]: both passages put the answer *in a feeling, within*. Same thread, same caveat: a feeling is meaningful, but not infallible.
- **This vault's own `active` rule** comes close to this passage: "The truth does not come from memorizing a sentence or two." Collecting and understanding a quote is `insight` at most. Living it is something else. That is a real overlap, not just a surface resemblance.
- **The finger pointing at the moon**, an image from the Zen/Buddhist tradition: teachings are the finger, and mistaking the finger for the moon misses the point. It makes the same "point TO the truth" move.
- `inner-wisdom` ↔ `intuition`: here the feeling is a way of *knowing*, not a guide for *choosing*. That's why the primary tag is `inner-wisdom` and not `intuition`.
- `inner-wisdom` ↔ `thinking`: the limits of the intellect. This also touches Nguyen's distinction between trusting intuition and treating every feeling as infallible.
